Salary or Dividends? How to Pay Yourself From Your Corporation

For incorporated professionals and business owners, how you take money out of the company affects your taxes, your RRSP, and your CPP. Here's how the two options compare.

By Iberian Pacific Financial Services October 22, 2026 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Salary creates RRSP room and CPP benefits, and is deductible to the corporation.
  • Dividends are simpler and avoid CPP, but don't create RRSP room.
  • Canada's tax system aims to make the total tax on both roughly similar.
  • Many owners use a mix, reviewed each year with their accountant.

One of the first decisions an incorporated professional or business owner faces is how to pay themselves. The two main options are salary and dividends, and each has trade-offs that go well beyond this year's tax bill.

Paying yourself a salary

  • Deductible to the corporation: salary reduces the company's taxable income.
  • Creates RRSP room: salary counts as earned income, which builds RRSP contribution room for the following year.
  • Builds CPP: you contribute to the Canada Pension Plan on both the employer and employee side, which costs more today but earns a lifetime, inflation-indexed pension later.
  • Helps with other benefits: a steady salary can make it easier to qualify for a mortgage, personal disability insurance, and the childcare expense deduction.
  • More administration: you'll need payroll, source deductions, and T4 slips.

Paying yourself dividends

  • Paid from after-tax profits: the corporation pays tax first, then you pay personal tax on the dividend, reduced by the dividend tax credit.
  • No CPP: you save the contributions now, but you won't build CPP retirement benefits.
  • No RRSP room: dividends aren't earned income, so they don't create RRSP room.
  • Simpler: no payroll is required.
  • Watch the family rules: since 2018, the tax on split income (TOSI) rules limit the benefit of paying dividends to family members who aren't actively involved in the business.

Why the totals are often close

Canada's tax system is designed around a principle called integration: the combined corporate and personal tax on dividends should be roughly the same as the personal tax on an equal salary. In practice it isn't perfect, so the right mix depends on your province, your income, and your goals.

Leaving money in the corporation

Profits you don't need personally can stay in the company and be invested, taxed at the lower small business rate. Keep an eye on passive investment income: once it passes $50,000 a year, the corporation's access to the small business rate starts to shrink, and it's eliminated at $150,000. Planning how those investments are held, and whether corporate-owned life insurance fits, can make a real difference.

A common approach

Many owners pay enough salary to maximize RRSP room and build CPP, then top up with dividends. Others prefer dividends for simplicity and invest the CPP savings themselves. The right answer changes as your income, family, and goals change, so it's worth reviewing every year with your accountant and advisor.
