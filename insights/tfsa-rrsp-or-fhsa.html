TFSA, RRSP, or FHSA: Where Should Your Next Dollar Go?

Canada's three main registered accounts each save tax in a different way. Here's how to decide which one comes first for you.

By Iberian Pacific Financial Services October 22, 2026 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A TFSA gives no deduction today, but growth and withdrawals are tax-free.
  • An RRSP gives a deduction today; withdrawals are taxed later.
  • An FHSA combines both for first-time home buyers, within set limits.
  • Your tax rate today compared with later is often the deciding factor.

"Should I put money in my TFSA or my RRSP?" is one of the most common questions we hear, and for first-time buyers there's now a third option: the First Home Savings Account. Each one saves tax, just at a different point in time.

The Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA)

Contributions aren't tax-deductible, but everything inside grows tax-free and withdrawals are never taxed. If you take money out, that amount is added back to your contribution room on January 1 of the following year. Room builds every year from age 18, and unused room carries forward.

Often best for: flexible goals, emergency savings, and people whose income, and tax rate, is lower today than it will be later.

The Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP)

Contributions are tax-deductible, so you get a tax refund today, and investments grow tax-deferred. Withdrawals are taxed as income. Your room is 18% of the previous year's earned income, up to an annual maximum, and unused room carries forward. The Home Buyers' Plan lets first-time buyers withdraw up to $60,000 for a home and repay it over time.

Often best for: higher-income years, and when you expect a lower tax rate in retirement. If your employer matches RRSP contributions, that match is usually the first place your money should go.

The First Home Savings Account (FHSA)

The FHSA gives you a deduction like an RRSP and tax-free withdrawals like a TFSA, as long as the money is used to buy a qualifying first home. You can contribute $8,000 a year, up to $40,000 in total, and carry forward up to $8,000 of unused room. You generally qualify if you haven't lived in a home you owned in the current year or the previous four years. If you don't end up buying, the balance can usually be transferred to your RRSP without using your RRSP room.

Often best for: anyone who might buy a first home. Because of the double tax benefit, it's hard to beat.

A simple way to decide

  1. Capture any employer match in your workplace plan first.
  2. Thinking about a first home? Open an FHSA, even with a small amount, so your room starts building.
  3. Compare your tax rate today with your expected rate later. Higher today usually favours the RRSP; lower today usually favours the TFSA.
  4. Need flexibility? Keep your emergency fund and shorter-term goals in a TFSA.
Why the rate matters

If you contribute to an RRSP while your tax rate is 40% and withdraw when it's 30%, you come out ahead of a TFSA. If the rates are the same, the two end up roughly equal, as long as you reinvest your RRSP refund.
