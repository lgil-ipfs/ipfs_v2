Keeping the Family Cottage: Planning for the Tax Bill

Many families hope to pass the cottage on for generations. Without a plan for the capital gains tax, it can end up being sold instead.

By Iberian Pacific Financial Services October 22, 2026 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • At death, property is treated as if it were sold at today's value.
  • Half of the capital gain is taxable, which can mean a large bill.
  • A spouse can usually inherit without tax, delaying the bill.
  • Life insurance and early planning can keep the cottage in the family.

For many families, the cottage is where summers happen, where grandparents taught the kids to swim, and where everyone comes back to. It's also often one of the most valuable things a family owns, and that creates a tax problem many people don't see coming.

What happens to the cottage at death

When someone passes away, the Canada Revenue Agency treats their property as if it had been sold at its fair market value. This is called a deemed disposition. If the cottage has grown in value, the gain is taxed on the owner's final tax return, and half of a capital gain is taxable.

The cottage tax bill

A cottage bought for $200,000 is now worth $1,000,000. That's an $800,000 capital gain, and $400,000 of it is taxable. At a tax rate close to 50%, the estate could owe about $200,000.

That bill has to be paid before the estate is settled. If there's no cash or insurance set aside, the family's only option may be to sell the cottage to pay the tax. The place that held a lifetime of memories leaves the family for good.

The spousal rollover

If the cottage passes to a spouse or common-law partner, the tax can usually be deferred until that person passes away. The bill doesn't disappear; it's just delayed until the second death, often when the children inherit.

The principal residence exemption

A family can generally shelter one property per year from capital gains tax as its principal residence, and a cottage can qualify. Using the exemption on the cottage means the city home can't use it for those years, so the decision depends on which property has grown more. This is worth working through carefully with an accountant.

Ways families keep the cottage

  • Life insurance: a policy sized to the expected tax bill, often a joint policy that pays out on the second death, gives the estate cash to pay the tax so the cottage can stay.
  • Keeping records: the original purchase price plus major improvements, like a new roof or an addition, raise the cost base and lower the taxable gain. Save the receipts.
  • Transferring during your lifetime: gifting the cottage to your children, or moving it into a trust, can lock in today's value, but it can also trigger tax now and comes with its own rules.
  • A family agreement: deciding in advance who will own it, who pays for upkeep, and what happens if someone wants out prevents conflict later.
